core_principle: >
  The product is a baton passed hand to hand through a chain of distinct
  worlds, each with its own visual language, until it reaches the person who
  needs it. Every handoff crosses a border between styles, eras, or media,
  and the journey itself says how far people will go to deliver this product.
consumer_psychology: >
  Delight in recognition and momentum: each new world rewards the viewer for
  knowing it, the handoffs build forward motion like a chase, and the final
  delivery turns the product into a gift that the whole world conspired to
  bring.
story_structure: >
  A grounded protagonist with a small need (tired, blocked, thirsty), the
  product discovered in the first world, a relay of three to six handoffs
  across clearly different worlds with one obstacle or gag per leg, the
  arrival in the protagonist's hand, the product solving the need, and a
  closing wink from one of the worlds.
hook_methods: >
  Show the first world breaking its own rule within seconds: a figure in a
  painting reaches out of the frame, a cartoon hand comes through a screen, a
  statue turns its head toward the product.
product_role: >
  The product is the only object that keeps its real form in every world. It
  is the reason for the journey and the thing every character protects, and
  the ending shows it doing its everyday job for an ordinary person.
suitable_categories_and_goals: >
  Iconic consumer brands with a recognizable pack or silhouette: soft drinks,
  snacks, chocolate, beer, coffee, sneakers, phones, delivery and travel
  services. Brand love, cultural relevance, launch films, and campaigns that
  want to show many styles in one piece.
unsuitable_cases: >
  Products without a recognizable physical form, short formats under fifteen
  seconds where the relay cannot breathe, and categories where whimsy
  undermines trust such as medical, legal, or financial services.
common_mistakes: >
  Worlds that look alike so the borders are not felt; handoffs without a
  physical action linking frames; the product changing its look in each
  world; too many legs so none of them lands; using copyrighted artworks or
  characters without clearance; forgetting the human need that motivates the
  relay.
cross_category_examples: >
  Cola: a bottle in a pop-art print is passed through a cubist portrait, a stormy seascape, a Van Gogh bedroom and an ukiyo-e snow scene to a tired art student, who drinks it and starts drawing.
  Sneakers: a shoe is thrown from a graffiti wall into a comic strip, then into a pixel game, then into a sports broadcast, landing on a runner's foot at the start line.
  Food delivery: a bag travels through a noir film, a cartoon, a wuxia rooftop and a sci-fi corridor to reach a gamer who has not left his chair.

# World-Hopping Relay

The product travels; the worlds change. Each leg is a short film in a
different style, joined by one continuous object passed from hand to hand.

## Why It Works

Style changes are usually a montage; a relay gives them a cause and a
direction. The viewer follows the product the way they would follow a ball in
a match, and the arrival feels earned.

## How To Execute

1. Define the protagonist's small, human need and the moment of delivery.
2. Choose three to six worlds with maximally different visual languages and
   order them for contrast and escalation.
3. Design each handoff as a physical action that crosses the border between
   frames: reaching, throwing, falling, sliding, swimming.
4. Give each leg one obstacle or gag that belongs to that world.
5. Keep the product's real form, color, and logo constant in every world.
6. End with the product doing its everyday job, then one world winks back.
7. Pair each leg with a matching video style skill, and clear rights for any
   real artworks or characters.
