# One recognizable person before and after the mishap

Preserve the approved identity, hair, outfit, footwear and accessories across
all references. Use the existing Main and full-body multi-view contracts.
References establish anatomy, proportion and usable clothing construction;
they do not need to enact the accident or contain flying products.
The carrying pose is a starting state, not a permanent identity feature. Use the
bound product reference for the advertised item's design rather than an
incidental substitute in a character sheet. Later release or pickup changes
possession without changing the wearer's identity or creating another item.

Establish carrying relationships for the three distinct belongings: which hand
holds each item, which item is worn, where a
bag strap rests, which side wears an accessory, and how fingers can release a
held item. Use available hands and real attachment points; three items do not
mean three hands. Release follows contact, never an unexplained accessory jump.
Keep a phone's screen and grip functionally oriented. Preserve separate
identities for the protagonist and passerby. Do not add a duplicated body during
an over-shoulder view or a product close-up.

Choose an everyday walk and a legible startle compatible with the approved
character. Performance may be exaggerated: a reaching hand, torso lean or low
look can resolve the mishap. Match the narrative handoff; falling to the ground
is optional, not a required identity pose for reference images. The story does not require a specific gender, body type, pink dress
or fashion pose. Keep explicitly stylized characters in their selected medium;
realistic identity references do not determine the final film medium.
